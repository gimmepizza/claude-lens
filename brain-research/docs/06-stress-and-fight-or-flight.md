# Stress, the Fight-or-Flight Response, and Emotion

*A mechanistic reference on how the brain and body detect threat, mobilize for action, and recover — and how these systems relate to emotion and health.*

---

## Introduction

Every organism must solve a recurring problem: how to detect danger and marshal the body's resources fast enough to survive it, then stand down and repair once the danger has passed. In vertebrates, and especially in mammals, this problem is solved by an integrated set of neural and endocrine systems that together constitute the **stress response**. When you swerve to avoid a collision, feel your heart pound before a performance, or freeze at an unexpected noise, you are witnessing the output of circuitry that is hundreds of millions of years old.

The stress response is not a single event but a coordinated cascade unfolding across two very different timescales. A **fast, neural wave** — the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) axis — reaches full effect in seconds and floods the body with adrenaline. A **slower, hormonal wave** — the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis — peaks over minutes and releases cortisol, which sustains and eventually resolves the response. Layered on top of these are the brain's threat-detection and regulation circuits (the amygdala and prefrontal cortex) and the broader repertoire of defensive behaviors (fight, flight, freeze, and fawn).

This document traces that machinery from anatomy through mechanism to health consequences, and closes with the major theories that connect bodily arousal to the subjective experience of emotion. Throughout, the emphasis is on **mechanism and time course**: what triggers what, through which structures, and how quickly.

> **A note on terminology.** "Stress" in physiology refers to a stimulus (a *stressor*) that threatens homeostasis, and to the body's response to it. The response is adaptive and life-saving in the acute setting; the damage attributed to "stress" in everyday language comes mostly from **chronic** activation of these same adaptive systems.

## 1. The Autonomic Nervous System

The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** is the division of the peripheral nervous system that governs involuntary bodily functions — heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, digestion, glandular secretion, pupil diameter, and sexual arousal — largely below the level of conscious control. It is the final common pathway through which the brain's stress circuitry acts on the body. The ANS is classically divided into three parts: the **sympathetic**, the **parasympathetic**, and the **enteric** nervous systems.

### 1.1 The two-neuron chain

A hallmark of autonomic motor output is that it uses a **two-neuron chain** from the central nervous system to the target organ, unlike the single motor neuron of the somatic (voluntary) system. A **preganglionic neuron** with its cell body in the brainstem or spinal cord synapses in a peripheral **ganglion** onto a **postganglionic neuron**, which then projects to the effector organ. This arrangement allows a single central command to be amplified and distributed across many targets.

### 1.2 The sympathetic division — "fight or flight"

The sympathetic division mobilizes the body for action requiring rapid, energetic responses. Its defining features:

- **Thoracolumbar outflow.** Preganglionic cell bodies sit in the **lateral horn** of the spinal cord from segments **T1 to L2–L3**. Fibers exit via the ventral roots and pass through the **white rami communicantes** (white because myelinated) to reach the sympathetic chain.
- **The sympathetic chain (paravertebral ganglia / sympathetic trunk).** Two chains of interconnected ganglia run parallel to the vertebral column from the base of the skull to the sacrum. A preganglionic fiber entering the chain may synapse at its level, travel up or down to synapse at another level, or pass through to a **prevertebral (collateral) ganglion** (e.g., the celiac or superior mesenteric ganglion). Postganglionic fibers rejoin spinal nerves through the **gray rami communicantes** (gray because unmyelinated) to reach sweat glands, arrector pili muscles, and blood vessels of the body wall.
- **Short pre-, long postganglionic fibers.** Because the ganglia lie close to the cord, preganglionic fibers are short and postganglionic fibers long — and one preganglionic neuron diverges onto many postganglionic neurons, producing the diffuse, mass activation characteristic of sympathetic arousal.
- **Chemistry.** All preganglionic fibers release **acetylcholine** (acting on nicotinic receptors). Most postganglionic sympathetic fibers are **adrenergic**, releasing **noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** onto adrenergic receptors. The important exception: sweat glands receive **cholinergic** sympathetic fibers.
- **The adrenal medulla — a modified ganglion.** Uniquely, preganglionic sympathetic fibers pass through the chain without synapsing and travel to the **adrenal medulla**, whose **chromaffin cells** are effectively modified postganglionic neurons. When stimulated, they dump hormones (mostly **adrenaline/epinephrine**, with some noradrenaline) directly into the bloodstream. This is the anatomical basis of the SAM axis (Section 2).

### 1.3 The parasympathetic division — "rest and digest"

The parasympathetic division conserves energy and supports maintenance functions: digestion, glandular secretion, slowing of the heart, and restoration. Its features contrast point-for-point with the sympathetic:

- **Craniosacral outflow.** Preganglionic neurons originate in the brainstem nuclei of cranial nerves **III (oculomotor), VII (facial), IX (glossopharyngeal), and X (vagus)**, and in the **sacral** spinal cord (**S2–S4**).
- **Long pre-, short postganglionic fibers.** Parasympathetic ganglia lie in or near the target organ, so preganglionic fibers are long and postganglionic fibers short. Divergence is low, giving parasympathetic effects a more **discrete, organ-specific** character than the sympathetic mass response.
- **Chemistry.** Both pre- and postganglionic parasympathetic fibers are **cholinergic** — preganglionic fibers act on nicotinic receptors, postganglionic fibers on **muscarinic** receptors in the target tissue.
- **The vagus nerve (cranial nerve X).** The vagus is the longest and most far-ranging cranial nerve, carrying roughly **75% of all parasympathetic fibers**. It "wanders" (Latin *vagus*) from the medulla through the neck and thorax into the abdomen, innervating the heart, lungs, and most of the gastrointestinal tract to the level of the transverse colon. Critically, the vagus is **~80% afferent (sensory)**: most of its fibers carry information *from* the viscera *to* the brain, providing the interoceptive signals that inform emotion and homeostatic regulation. Its efferent slowing of the heart is the physiological basis of **vagal tone** (Section 8).

### 1.4 The enteric division — the "second brain"

The **enteric nervous system (ENS)** is a semi-autonomous network embedded in the wall of the gastrointestinal tract, containing on the order of **200–600 million neurons** — comparable to the spinal cord. It comprises two major plexuses: the **myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus**, which controls gut motility, and the **submucosal (Meissner's) plexus**, which regulates secretion and local blood flow, together with the pacemaker **interstitial cells of Cajal**. The ENS can coordinate reflexes such as peristalsis independently of the brain and spinal cord, but it is modulated by sympathetic (inhibitory) and parasympathetic (excitatory) input. Its dense two-way communication with the brain via the vagus — the "gut-brain axis" — helps explain why stress so reliably disrupts digestion.

### 1.5 The autonomic balance

At rest, the two divisions are not simply "on" or "off"; both maintain a continuous baseline tone, and organs are governed by the **balance** between them. Most viscera receive **dual innervation** with opposing effects — sympathetic activity accelerates the heart, parasympathetic activity slows it. This push-pull arrangement lets the body fine-tune organ function moment to moment. The stress response is, at its core, a decisive shift of this balance toward sympathetic dominance, followed (in health) by a swing back toward parasympathetic recovery.

## 2. The Fight-or-Flight Response: The Fast (SAM) Axis

The term **"fight-or-flight"** was coined by the physiologist **Walter Cannon** in the early 20th century to describe the body's rapid mobilization in the face of a threat. It is the fast, neurally-driven first wave of the stress response, and it reaches full physiological effect within **seconds**.

### 2.1 Step by step

1. **Perception of threat.** Sensory information (a looming shape, a sudden sound, a smell) reaches the **thalamus**, the brain's sensory relay. From here it takes two routes (see Section 5): a fast, crude "low road" straight to the amygdala, and a slower, detailed "high road" through the sensory cortex.
2. **Amygdala triggers the alarm.** The **amygdala**, the brain's threat-detection hub, evaluates the input for danger — often *before* conscious awareness. On detecting threat, it signals the hypothalamus.
3. **Hypothalamus activates the sympathetic outflow.** The **hypothalamus** (particularly its paraventricular nucleus and connections to the brainstem) commands the sympathetic nervous system, sending signals down the spinal cord to the preganglionic sympathetic neurons.
4. **Signal to the adrenal medulla.** Preganglionic sympathetic fibers travel (via the greater splanchnic nerve) directly to the **adrenal medulla**, synapsing on its chromaffin cells.
5. **Adrenaline release.** The chromaffin cells secrete **adrenaline (epinephrine)** — roughly 80% of the output — and **noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** into the bloodstream. Simultaneously, direct sympathetic nerves release noradrenaline onto organs throughout the body.
6. **Body-wide mobilization.** Circulating adrenaline plus direct sympathetic innervation act on adrenergic receptors across the body to produce the coordinated changes tabulated below.

The entire sequence — from threat perception to hormone release and a pounding heart — unfolds in **a matter of seconds**, which is precisely what a survival-critical response demands.

### 2.2 The physiological changes and their logic

Every change serves a single overarching purpose: **deliver oxygen and glucose to the brain and skeletal muscles, and defer any function that can wait.**

| System / organ | Change during fight-or-flight | Mediator(s) | Adaptive purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heart rate** | Increases (tachycardia) | β1-adrenergic (NE, Epi) | Pumps more blood to muscles and brain |
| **Force of heart contraction** | Increases | β1-adrenergic | Raises cardiac output |
| **Blood pressure** | Rises | Vasoconstriction + ↑ cardiac output | Sustains perfusion pressure |
| **Blood flow distribution** | Shunted to skeletal muscle, heart, brain; away from skin and gut | β2 (dilation in muscle) / α1 (constriction elsewhere) | Prioritizes organs of action; limits bleeding from skin wounds |
| **Bronchi / airways** | Dilate (bronchodilation) | β2-adrenergic | Maximizes air intake / oxygen uptake |
| **Respiratory rate** | Increases | Central + sympathetic | More oxygen in, more CO₂ out |
| **Pupils** | Dilate (mydriasis) | α1-adrenergic (radial muscle) | Admits more light; widens visual field |
| **Liver / blood glucose** | Glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis raise blood glucose | Adrenaline; later cortisol | Quick fuel for muscle and brain |
| **Adipose tissue** | Lipolysis releases free fatty acids | β-adrenergic | Additional energy substrate |
| **Skeletal muscle** | Increased tension, tremor; glycogen breakdown | Sympathetic | Readiness to act |
| **Digestion** | Motility and secretion inhibited; sphincters contract; salivation drops (dry mouth) | Sympathetic inhibition of ENS | Diverts energy away from a non-urgent task |
| **Sweat glands** | Activated (palms, etc.) | Cholinergic sympathetic | Thermoregulation; improved grip |
| **Blood clotting** | Enhanced | Adrenaline, platelet effects | Limits blood loss from injury |
| **Immune cells** | Redistributed (acute mobilization) | Catecholamines, cortisol | Positions immune defense against wounding/infection |
| **Bladder** | Detrusor relaxes, sphincter contracts (retention) | Sympathetic | Defers elimination |
| **Piloerection** | Hair stands up ("goosebumps") | α-adrenergic (arrector pili) | Vestigial threat display in furred ancestors |

Subjectively, this constellation is experienced as a racing heart, rapid breathing, dry mouth, sweaty palms, dilated vision, tense muscles, and a "butterflies" feeling in the stomach — the felt signature of sympathetic arousal.

## 3. The HPA Axis: The Slow Hormonal Wave

If the SAM axis is the body's sprinter, the **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis** is its long-distance runner. It is an endocrine cascade that begins seconds after the stressor but takes **minutes** to reach peak effect, and it sustains and ultimately resolves the response over minutes to hours.

### 3.1 The cascade

1. **Hypothalamus → CRH.** Neurons in the **paraventricular nucleus (PVN)** of the hypothalamus release **corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)** (along with **vasopressin/AVP**, which potentiates it) into the hypophyseal portal circulation — a private set of blood vessels connecting the hypothalamus to the anterior pituitary.
2. **Pituitary → ACTH.** CRH stimulates **corticotroph** cells of the **anterior pituitary** to release **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)** into the general circulation. (ACTH is cleaved from a larger precursor, pro-opiomelanocortin, POMC.)
3. **Adrenal cortex → cortisol.** ACTH travels through the bloodstream to the **adrenal cortex** (specifically the *zona fasciculata*) and stimulates synthesis and release of **cortisol** (a glucocorticoid; corticosterone is the rodent equivalent). Blood cortisol typically peaks roughly **15–30 minutes** after stressor onset.

Note the anatomical elegance: the **adrenal gland has two functionally distinct parts**, both engaged by stress. The inner **medulla** (fast, neural) releases adrenaline; the outer **cortex** (slow, hormonal) releases cortisol.

### 3.2 What cortisol does

Cortisol is a lipophilic steroid that crosses cell membranes and binds intracellular **glucocorticoid receptors (GR)** and **mineralocorticoid receptors (MR)**, altering gene transcription. Its actions extend and consolidate the mobilization begun by adrenaline, and prepare the body for a prolonged challenge:

- **Raises blood glucose** by stimulating gluconeogenesis in the liver and reducing glucose uptake in peripheral tissues (an anti-insulin effect) — ensuring a sustained fuel supply for the brain.
- **Mobilizes energy stores** — promoting protein breakdown (proteolysis) in muscle and fat breakdown (lipolysis) to supply gluconeogenic substrates.
- **Modulates the immune system** — acutely repositioning immune cells, but broadly **suppressing inflammation** at higher/sustained levels (the basis for pharmaceutical corticosteroids).
- **Supports cardiovascular tone** — permitting catecholamines to act effectively on the vasculature (a "permissive" effect).
- **Acts on the brain** — influencing arousal, mood, appetite, and — importantly — memory (Section 7). The hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex are all rich in corticosteroid receptors.

Cortisol is not solely a "stress hormone." It follows a strong **circadian rhythm**, peaking in the early morning (the *cortisol awakening response*) and falling to a nadir around midnight, orchestrating daily metabolism and arousal.

### 3.3 Negative feedback — the built-in brake

The HPA axis is self-limiting through **negative feedback**. Rising cortisol acts back on the **pituitary** (suppressing ACTH) and the **hypothalamus** (suppressing CRH), and also on the **hippocampus**, which exerts inhibitory control over the PVN. This closed loop shuts off cortisol production once it has risen — the hormone turns off its own signal. Effective feedback is what allows the stress response to be **switched off** when the threat passes; its failure is a central feature of chronic stress and of disorders such as depression and PTSD, where the "off switch" becomes unreliable.

## 4. Beyond Fight-or-Flight: Freeze, Fawn, and the Defense Cascade

"Fight-or-flight" captures only part of the defensive repertoire. Evolution has equipped animals with a graded series of innate defenses — often called the **defense cascade** — selected according to the nature and proximity of the threat. The popular formulation is **"fight, flight, freeze, and fawn."**

### 4.1 The defensive options

- **Freeze (attentive immobility).** On first detecting a distant or ambiguous threat, the animal halts, orients, and heightens vigilance — the "freeze to assess" state. Paradoxically, heart rate often *drops* here (fear bradycardia) via vagal engagement, even as attention sharpens. Freezing reduces the chance of being detected by a predator whose vision is tuned to movement.
- **Flight.** If escape is possible, the animal runs. Flight is favored when a route to safety exists.
- **Fight.** If cornered and escape is impossible, the animal attacks defensively — the last active option.
- **Tonic immobility ("playing dead," collapsed immobility).** When threat is **inescapable and overwhelming** — for example, being physically restrained by a predator or, in humans, during assault — the body may enter **tonic immobility**: a state of profound motor inhibition, reduced responsiveness, analgesia, and sometimes a sudden parasympathetic surge (which can cause fainting). This is a response of *last resort*, not a choice, and its involuntary nature is important clinically: survivors of assault who "froze" often carry misplaced guilt for not fighting back.
- **Fawn (appease/tend-and-befriend).** A more recently emphasized, socially-mediated response (popularized by therapist **Pete Walker**) in which an individual attempts to defuse a threat by placating, appeasing, or complying with the aggressor. It overlaps conceptually with the **"tend-and-befriend"** response described by Shelley Taylor, an affiliative pattern especially prominent under stress.

### 4.2 The periaqueductal gray

The **periaqueductal gray (PAG)** — a column of gray matter surrounding the cerebral aqueduct in the midbrain — is the key brainstem coordinator of these defensive behaviors and their autonomic accompaniments (it is also a hub for pain modulation). Different PAG subdivisions map onto different defenses:

- The **dorsal / lateral PAG** drives **active defense** — the fight and flight behaviors and the accompanying sympathetic surge and analgesia. Threat proximity matters: distal threats engage forebrain circuits, but an imminent threat shifts control toward the PAG for a fast, reflexive escape.
- The **ventrolateral PAG (vlPAG)** drives **passive defense** — **freezing and tonic immobility**, working with vagal (parasympathetic) pathways from the dorsal motor nucleus to slow the heart.

The **amygdala** sits upstream, selecting the defensive mode and driving the appropriate PAG column: its projections to the vlPAG produce freezing, while other outputs favor active escape. Thus the same threat-detection hub can produce opposite behaviors — bursting into flight or locking into stillness — depending on which downstream circuit it recruits.

## 5. The Amygdala, Fear, and Prefrontal Regulation

### 5.1 The amygdala as threat detector

The **amygdala** is an almond-shaped cluster of nuclei deep in each temporal lobe. It is the brain's central hub for detecting biologically significant stimuli — above all, threat — and for orchestrating fear responses. Two of its nuclei are especially important:

- The **basolateral complex (BLA)** receives sensory input and is where stimulus-danger associations are formed (fear learning).
- The **central nucleus (CeA)** is the major *output* station, projecting to the hypothalamus (autonomic and endocrine responses), the PAG (defensive behavior), and brainstem arousal centers.

Through these outputs, the amygdala can simultaneously trigger the SAM axis, the HPA axis, and defensive behavior — coordinating the whole-body response described above.

### 5.2 The "low road" and the "high road" (LeDoux)

The neuroscientist **Joseph LeDoux** described two parallel pathways by which sensory information reaches the amygdala:

- **The "low road."** A fast, direct route from the **sensory thalamus straight to the amygdala**. It carries a crude, low-resolution version of the stimulus but arrives quickly — allowing a fear response to begin *before* conscious identification of the threat. This is the "quick and dirty" pathway that makes you jump back from a coiled rope before you realize it is not a snake.
- **The "high road."** A slower route from the thalamus through the **sensory cortex** (and prefrontal cortex) and then to the amygdala. It delivers a detailed, contextualized analysis that can confirm or cancel the alarm.

The low road's evolutionary logic is stark: the cost of a false alarm (flinching at a rope) is trivial next to the cost of a missed real threat (a snake). Speed is bought at the price of accuracy, and the cortical high road corrects the errors a moment later.

### 5.3 Fear conditioning and extinction

**Fear conditioning** is the associative learning by which a neutral stimulus (a tone) that reliably precedes an aversive event (a shock) comes itself to elicit fear. Sensory inputs about the tone and the shock **converge in the basolateral amygdala**, where synaptic plasticity (long-term potentiation) forges the association; thereafter the tone alone drives the CeA and its fear outputs. This is among the best-understood examples of learning at the synaptic level.

**Extinction** is the *new learning* that occurs when the conditioned stimulus is presented repeatedly *without* the aversive outcome, so the fear response wanes. Crucially, extinction does not erase the original fear memory — it lays down a competing inhibitory memory, which is why fears can spontaneously recover or be reinstated. Extinction is the mechanistic basis of **exposure therapy**, the most effective treatment for phobias, panic disorder, and PTSD.

### 5.4 The prefrontal cortex as regulator

The **medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)** — in humans, especially the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC)** — exerts top-down control over the amygdala. During extinction learning, the vmPFC builds new inhibitory connections onto the amygdala; as extinction proceeds, **amygdala activation falls while vmPFC activation rises**, and the strength of this reciprocal, anticorrelated relationship predicts how well fear is suppressed. The vmPFC effectively signals "this context is safe" and applies the brakes.

This regulatory circuit is fragile. Acute stress and high cortisol impair prefrontal function while *enhancing* amygdala reactivity — a shift that biases the brain toward reflexive, fear-driven responding precisely when danger is present. When this imbalance becomes chronic (an overactive amygdala poorly restrained by a hypoactive prefrontal cortex), it is a core feature of anxiety disorders and PTSD (Section 7).

## 6. Acute vs. Chronic Stress: Allostasis and Allostatic Load

### 6.1 Acute stress is adaptive

The systems described above are **protective**. A well-functioning stress response mobilizes energy, sharpens attention and memory for the threat, primes the immune system against wounding, and then — critically — **switches off**. Acute, time-limited stress with efficient recovery is not harmful; it is how the body meets challenges.

### 6.2 Allostasis and allostatic load

The concept of **allostasis** — "stability through change" — reframes homeostasis: rather than holding internal variables at fixed set-points, the body *actively adjusts* set-points (blood pressure, glucose, cortisol) to meet anticipated demand. Allostasis is achieved precisely through the mediators of stress: catecholamines and glucocorticoids.

The problem is cost. **Bruce McEwen and Eliot Stellar (1993)** introduced **allostatic load** to denote the **cumulative wear-and-tear** that accumulates when these adaptive systems are called upon too often, fail to shut off, or respond inadequately. McEwen identified several patterns that generate load:

1. **Frequent stress** — repeated activation of the response.
2. **Failure to habituate** — mounting a full response to a repeated stressor instead of adapting.
3. **Failure to shut off** — the response continuing after the stressor ends (impaired negative feedback).
4. **Inadequate response** — an under-active axis (e.g., low cortisol) that fails to restrain other systems such as inflammation, so *those* systems overcompensate.

When load becomes extreme, McEwen termed it **allostatic overload** — the tipping point at which the mediators that once protected begin to damage.

### 6.3 Health consequences of chronic stress

Chronically elevated (or dysregulated) catecholamines and cortisol drive a broad, well-replicated pattern of pathology:

- **Cardiovascular.** Sustained high blood pressure, endothelial injury, and accelerated **atherosclerosis**; increased risk of hypertension, heart attack, and stroke.
- **Metabolic.** Insulin resistance, visceral (abdominal) fat deposition, dyslipidemia — the components of **metabolic syndrome** and type 2 diabetes.
- **Immune.** A shift from short-term immune enhancement to chronic **immunosuppression** and, paradoxically, **low-grade chronic inflammation**, impairing wound healing and defense against infection.
- **Bone and other tissues.** Bone demineralization; muscle wasting from sustained proteolysis.
- **Brain.** This is where the effects are most striking. Under repeated stress, neurons of the **hippocampus** and **prefrontal cortex** undergo dendritic **atrophy** and reduced neurogenesis, whereas neurons of the **amygdala** show dendritic **growth (hypertrophy)**. The net structural shift — a weakened hippocampus/PFC and a strengthened amygdala — biases the brain toward heightened fear and anxiety and impaired regulation and memory. In humans, elevated cortisol during aging predicts **hippocampal shrinkage and memory decline**.

Thus the very hormones essential for survival in the short run **exact a cost over the long run** — the central paradox of stress physiology.

## 7. Stress and Memory; PTSD

### 7.1 The inverted-U relationship

The effect of stress hormones on memory is famously **non-monotonic**, following an **inverted-U (Yerkes-Dodson-like) curve**: *moderate* levels of arousal and glucocorticoids **enhance** memory, whereas *very low* or *very high* levels **impair** it. A little stress helps you remember; overwhelming stress fragments memory.

But the story is stage- and structure-dependent:

- **Consolidation (forming new memories).** Glucocorithoids, acting together with adrenaline and the amygdala, **enhance the consolidation** of emotionally arousing memories — which is why you vividly remember frightening or exciting events. The BLA is essential: it modulates hippocampal consolidation so that emotional events are preferentially stamped in.
- **Retrieval and working memory.** Acutely elevated cortisol **impairs the retrieval** of already-stored memories and disrupts prefrontal **working memory** — the reason people "blank out" under acute stress (e.g., in an exam or on the witness stand).
- **Structure matters.** The inverted-U is most characteristic of **hippocampus-dependent** (explicit, contextual) memory. For strongly **amygdala-dependent** learning (simple cued fear conditioning), the relationship is more nearly **linear** — more stress, stronger memory — which contributes to the intrusive, overconsolidated emotional memories of trauma.

The combined result — enhanced emotional/amygdala memory but impaired contextual/hippocampal memory and retrieval — helps explain a paradox of trauma: the emotional core of the event is seared in and easily re-triggered, while its context (where, when, that it is over) is poorly bound, so the memory intrudes as if the danger were present *now*.

### 7.2 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

**PTSD** can develop after exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or violence. Its symptom clusters (per DSM-5) include **intrusion** (flashbacks, nightmares, intrusive memories), **avoidance** of reminders, **negative alterations in mood and cognition**, and **hyperarousal** (exaggerated startle, hypervigilance, sleep disturbance).

The best-supported neurobiological model is a **three-node network imbalance**:

- **Hyperactive amygdala** — an over-reactive threat detector, producing exaggerated fear responses to trauma reminders and even to neutral emotional stimuli.
- **Hypoactive medial/ventromedial prefrontal cortex** — impaired top-down inhibition, so the amygdala is poorly restrained and **extinction of fear fails** (the person cannot learn that reminders are now safe).
- **Smaller / dysfunctional hippocampus** — the most replicated structural finding in PTSD. A compromised hippocampus struggles to contextualize the memory in time and place, so traumatic recollections are re-experienced as present rather than past. Whether reduced hippocampal volume is a *cause* (a pre-existing vulnerability) or a *consequence* of glucocorticoid toxicity — or both — remains an active question.

This maps directly onto the fear-circuitry of Section 5: PTSD is, in large part, a disorder of **overactive threat detection** combined with **failed prefrontal regulation and failed extinction** — which is why extinction-based **exposure therapies** are frontline treatments.

## 8. Recovery: The Relaxation Response, Vagal Tone, and HRV

A stress response is only healthy if it can be **turned off**. Recovery is an active process driven by the parasympathetic nervous system.

### 8.1 The relaxation response

Cardiologist **Herbert Benson** described the **relaxation response** (1975) as a measurable physiological state that is essentially the mirror image of the fight-or-flight response: **decreased heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, muscle tension, and cortisol**, with a shift toward parasympathetic dominance. Unlike the stress response, it can be deliberately elicited — through slow breathing, meditation, progressive muscle relaxation, and similar practices — which is the physiological rationale for these interventions in stress management.

### 8.2 Vagal tone

**Vagal tone** is the level of parasympathetic (vagal) influence on the body, especially the heart. The vagus acts as a continuous **"brake"** on the heart's intrinsic pacemaker: with strong vagal tone the resting heart rate is lower and, critically, the parasympathetic brake can be **released quickly** to allow a rapid heart-rate increase when needed and **reapplied** to allow rapid recovery. High vagal tone is associated with better emotional regulation, flexible physiological responding, and health; low vagal tone is associated with poorer regulation and greater disease risk.

### 8.3 Heart rate variability (HRV)

**Heart rate variability** — the beat-to-beat variation in the intervals between heartbeats — is the most practical, non-invasive window onto autonomic balance. A healthy heart does **not** beat like a metronome; its rate speeds slightly on inhalation and slows on exhalation, a pattern called **respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA)**. Because this respiratory modulation is mediated by the vagus, **high-frequency HRV (RSA) is used as an index of cardiac vagal tone**. Higher HRV generally reflects a well-regulated, flexible autonomic system able to adapt to demands; chronically low HRV is a marker of stress, poor recovery, and elevated cardiovascular and psychiatric risk. HRV is stabilized by the **baroreflex**, the feedback loop by which pressure sensors in the great arteries adjust vagal output to buffer blood pressure.

### 8.4 A note on polyvagal theory

**Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory** proposed that the mammalian vagus has two functionally distinct branches — an evolutionarily older *unmyelinated* branch (dorsal motor nucleus) linked to immobilization/shutdown, and a newer *myelinated* branch (nucleus ambiguus) linked to calm social engagement — and it has been influential in trauma-informed clinical work. It is worth noting that several of the theory's specific evolutionary and mechanistic premises are **contested** in the physiology literature, even as the underlying finding — that **vagally-mediated HRV indexes self-regulation and social/emotional functioning** — remains robust and widely used.

## 9. Theories of Emotion and Modern Affective Neuroscience

The stress response raises a deep question: what is the relationship between the **bodily changes** of arousal and the **subjective feeling** of an emotion? The major theories offer different answers about the *order* and *causation* of stimulus, bodily response, and felt experience.

### 9.1 James-Lange theory

Proposed independently by **William James** and **Carl Lange** (~1884–1885), this theory reverses the intuitive order: an emotion-provoking stimulus first produces a **physiological/bodily reaction**, and the **feeling is our perception of that bodily change.** In James's formulation, we do not tremble because we are afraid — *we are afraid because we tremble*. Emotion is the mind reading the body. (Modern support: bodily feedback does shape emotional experience; interoception is central to feeling.)

### 9.2 Cannon-Bard theory

**Walter Cannon** and **Philip Bard** (1920s) objected that bodily states are too slow and too similar across different emotions to *be* the emotions. Their alternative: a stimulus is processed by the **thalamus**, which sends signals **simultaneously and independently** to the cortex (producing the conscious *feeling*) and to the body (producing the *physiological response*). Feeling and bodily arousal occur **at the same time**, neither causing the other.

### 9.3 Schachter-Singer two-factor theory

**Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962)** added **cognition**. In their **two-factor theory**, emotion requires *both* (1) general **physiological arousal** and (2) a **cognitive label** for that arousal derived from the situation. The same pounding heart is labeled "fear" on a rickety bridge but "attraction" in the presence of an appealing stranger. Arousal supplies the intensity; interpretation supplies the *identity* of the emotion.

### 9.4 Appraisal theory

**Appraisal theories** (notably **Magda Arnold** and **Richard Lazarus**) place **evaluation first**: emotion arises from how we *appraise* a situation's significance for our goals and well-being. Lazarus distinguished **primary appraisal** (Is this relevant? Is it threatening or benign?) from **secondary appraisal** (Do I have the resources to cope?). The appraisal determines the emotion and its bodily accompaniment — which is why the *same* event can frighten one person and excite another. Appraisal theory dominates much of modern stress psychology: a stressor is stressful largely to the degree it is *judged* to exceed one's coping resources.

### 9.5 Modern affective neuroscience

Contemporary neuroscience integrates and complicates these classical views:

- **Circuits, not a single seat.** Emotion emerges from distributed networks — the **amygdala**, **prefrontal cortex**, **insula**, **anterior cingulate**, **ventral striatum**, hypothalamus, and brainstem — rather than the thalamus alone. LeDoux's dual-pathway model (Section 5) shows that fast, non-conscious threat processing (low road) and slower cortical appraisal (high road) coexist, partially reconciling the "body-first" and "cognition-first" camps.
- **Interoception.** The **insula** integrates signals about the body's internal state; this interoceptive representation appears central to *feeling* emotions, giving a modern, neural grounding to James's insight.
- **Basic-emotion vs. constructionist views.** **Jaak Panksepp's affective neuroscience** argued for a small set of genetically-endowed, subcortically-based **primary emotional systems** — which he named SEEKING, FEAR, RAGE, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, and PLAY — conserved across mammals. In contrast, **Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion** holds that emotions are not hardwired categories but are *constructed* by the brain from core affect (valence and arousal) plus conceptual, contextual, and cultural knowledge — a view closely allied to appraisal and two-factor traditions.
- **A useful synthesis.** LeDoux himself distinguishes the *defensive survival circuits* (which produce the bodily fight-or-flight response non-consciously) from the *conscious feeling* of fear (which is assembled cortically). On this view the classical debate dissolves: the **body's stress response** and the **felt emotion** are related but separable products of overlapping systems — exactly the distinction that runs through this entire document.

## 10. Summary

- The **autonomic nervous system** governs the body's involuntary functions through three divisions — **sympathetic** (mobilizing), **parasympathetic** ("rest and digest," largely via the **vagus nerve**), and **enteric** (the gut's own network) — with organ function set by their moment-to-moment **balance**.
- The **fight-or-flight response** is the fast, neural first wave: threat → **amygdala** → **hypothalamus** → sympathetic outflow → **adrenal medulla** → **adrenaline/noradrenaline**, producing whole-body mobilization (↑ heart rate, blood pressure, bronchodilation, pupil dilation, glucose release, digestion shutdown) within **seconds**.
- The **HPA axis** is the slower hormonal wave: **CRH → ACTH → cortisol**, peaking over minutes, sustaining energy mobilization and immune modulation, and self-terminating through **negative feedback**.
- The full defensive repertoire — **fight, flight, freeze, fawn**, and **tonic immobility** — is coordinated by the **periaqueductal gray** under amygdala control.
- Fear is learned (conditioning) and unlearned (extinction) in the **amygdala**, and regulated top-down by the **prefrontal cortex**; failure of this regulation underlies anxiety disorders and **PTSD**.
- Acute stress is **adaptive**; the damage comes from **chronic** activation — **allostatic load** — which harms the cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune systems and shrinks the **hippocampus** while enlarging the **amygdala**.
- Cortisol's effect on memory follows an **inverted U**; recovery depends on **parasympathetic** re-engagement, indexed by **vagal tone** and **heart rate variability**.
- Classic **theories of emotion** (James-Lange, Cannon-Bard, Schachter-Singer, appraisal) each capture part of the stimulus-body-feeling relationship, which **modern affective neuroscience** reframes as the interaction of distributed brain circuits, interoception, and construction.
